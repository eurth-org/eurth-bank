A banknote is a small poster with three jobs. It has to say what it is worth at a glance, it has to say something about the country that issues it, and it has to be very hard to copy. Nearly everything on a note is there for at least one of those reasons, and the best notes make all three look like a single idea.

Bank draws notes for countries that don't exist. This guide explains the parts of real ones, so that yours look as if somebody planned them. Every feature below is explained; none is reproduced. Where Bank can draw its own version of a feature, the guide says so, and where it deliberately can't, it says that too.

## The anatomy of a banknote

### Front and back

The two sides are the front and the back; collectors and printers say obverse and reverse. The front usually carries the issuer's name, the main picture, the signatures and the serial number. The back carries a second picture and the value again. Both sides state the value in numerals, large and more than once, because a note is read in a hurry and in any direction.

In Bank, every design has both sides, and the back mirrors the layout of the front.

### The portrait

For two centuries the main picture on most notes has been a face: a monarch, a founder, a poet. A portrait says who the nation honours, and it is also a security feature. The usual explanation is that people are very good at noticing small differences in faces, so a portrait that has been copied badly looks wrong before anyone can say why.

Not every series has one. The current Swiss notes show no people at all, and Norway's replaced its portraits with the sea. The euro has no portraits either, since no single face could stand for so many countries at once.

In Bank, the portrait slot takes a silhouette of nobody in particular, or one of our buildings or landscapes, or the arms or flag from your nation's profile. It takes no photographs, of real people or of anyone else.

### The vignette

A vignette is a picture without a hard frame, engraved in fine lines: a building, a landscape, a ship, an animal. On many notes the back is one large vignette. Vignettes carry the theme of a series, and the choice of what to show is as political as the portrait. The euro's answer was to draw windows, doorways and bridges in the styles of different periods, and to make sure none of them was a real one, so that no country could claim a note.

In Bank, the back of the note has a framed vignette, drawn in the ink of that value.

### Guilloche and rosettes

Guilloche is the lace of banknotes: bands and rosettes made of fine lines that weave over and under each other with mechanical precision. It was first cut on geometric lathes, machines of gears and cams that move a cutting point through curves no hand could repeat. That was the point. An engraver could copy a portrait, given time, but not a pattern whose every line depends on the settings of a machine he doesn't have.

Today the patterns come from mathematics and software, and they still do the same work: lines so fine and so regular that a scanner or a printer turns them into mush.

In Bank, rosettes are generated from six numbers: how many petals, how many rings, how deep the petals cut, how strong a finer wave rides on them, how far each ring is turned against the next, and how many fine waves ride on each petal. The border can be a guilloche band as well. Nothing is traced from a real note; the same numbers always give the same rosette.

### Raised print

Run a fingernail across a paper banknote and you can often feel the ink. That is intaglio printing: the design is engraved into a metal plate, ink is forced into the grooves, and paper is pressed on under great pressure, so that the ink stands up from the surface. Intaglio gives the crisp lines of portraits and lettering, and a feel that ordinary printing can't imitate. Central banks tell the public to check a note three ways, and the first is to feel it.

Bank makes pictures, so there is nothing to feel. Its line art borrows the look of engraving and nothing more.

### Microprint

Microprint is lettering so small that it looks like a thin line until you take a magnifying glass to it. It usually repeats the name of the bank or the value. Copiers and home printers can't resolve it, so on a counterfeit the line is a blur.

In Bank, a hairline under the issuer's name repeats that name in capitals about a millimetre high. It is there to show where microprint goes on a note, and it is as legible as it looks.

### The watermark

A watermark is made in the paper itself, while it is still wet, by making the sheet a little thinner in some places and thicker in others. Held to the light, the thin parts shine and the thick parts stay dark, and a picture appears that is in the paper, not on it. It usually repeats the portrait or a motif of the series. No printer can add one afterwards.

Bank doesn't draw a watermark. A picture on a screen has no light behind it, and a fake watermark would be the one thing a watermark never is: printed.

### The security thread

The security thread is a thin strip embedded in the paper as it is made. Against the light it shows as a dark line, often with tiny lettering. A windowed thread comes to the surface at intervals, so that on the face of the note it looks like a row of metallic dashes, and against the light like one unbroken line.

Bank doesn't draw a thread either, for the same reason as the watermark.

### See-through marks, shifting inks and shiny things

A see-through register is a shape printed half on the front and half on the back, so exactly that the halves make one figure when the note is held to the light. Colour-shifting ink changes colour as the note is tilted. Holograms and foil stripes show moving images. Polymer notes add clear windows. A modern note has a dozen such features, some for the public, some for cash machines, and some that the central bank keeps to itself.

Many notes also carry marks meant for machines: patterns that scanners, copiers and image editors recognise, so that they refuse to work on a banknote. Bank draws none of these and never will. A tool for fiction has no business teaching a photocopier's worst habits.

### Serial numbers and signatures

Every real note has its own number, usually printed twice, with letters that say where and when it was made. The number lets a bank trace a note and lets a collector prize one. Signatures say who answers for the money: a governor, a chief cashier, a treasurer. Some notes carry one, many carry two.

In Bank, serial numbers follow a format you choose and come from a seed, so a note keeps its number every time it is drawn. Signatures are generated scribbles: a name is turned into a scrawl, and the same name always signs the same way. Nobody's real hand is copied.

### The value, twice

Notes state their value in numerals and in words. The numerals are for speed. The words are for certainty: it is easy to add a nought to a number, and much harder to turn "ten" into "one hundred".

In Bank, both are drawn for you, and a long value in words is wrapped and fitted so it never runs off the note.

## How a series is designed

A single note is a picture. A series is a system, and the system is where most of the design work goes.

### One theme

A series has a theme that every value interprets. The euro's is "ages and styles": each note shows the architecture of one period, from classical on the five to the iron and glass of the nineteenth century on the two hundred. The current Swiss series is called "The many facets of Switzerland", and gives each value a motif such as time, light, wind or water. Norway's eighth series is about the sea, with a lighthouse, a Viking ship and a cod on the fronts and a pattern of pixels on the backs that follows the Beaufort wind scale.

A theme is what lets six or seven notes look like a family. Pick one for your nation before you draw anything: rivers, crafts, constellations, the heroes of one war or the trees of one forest.

### A colour and a size for every value

In most series every value has its own main colour, so that a handful of notes can be told apart in a second. The euro's run grey, red, blue, orange, green and yellow-brown. Colours are chosen so that neighbouring values contrast as strongly as possible.

In many series every value also has its own size, growing with the value. The Swiss notes are all 70 millimetres tall and grow 7 millimetres longer with each value, from 123 millimetres for the ten to 158 for the thousand. Size sorts notes in a wallet, makes it harder to bleach a small note and print a large one on its paper, and matters most of all to people who can't see the colours.

In Bank, the whole series comes from one design: each value takes the next colours of a scheme and is a step longer and taller than the last. The schemes alternate lighter and darker inks, so that neighbouring values differ in lightness and not only in hue.

### Made for everyone

Central banks design with blind and partially sighted people, not just for them. Euro notes use different sizes, strong colours, large numerals set apart from the rest of the design, and raised lines on the edges whose number of breaks tells the value by touch. Good design for people who see badly turns out to be good design for everybody in a dark taxi.

On a screen, the same care means never relying on colour alone. Every note Bank draws states its value in numerals and words, and every picture comes with a written description.

### Which values

Most series follow the pattern 1, 2, 5: 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200. Each value is about double the last, which means any amount can be paid with few notes. Some countries skip the 2s and get by with 1, 5, 10, 50. Older series had threes and twenty-fives. A currency in trouble shows it here first: when prices run away, the bank prints higher and higher values, and the row of noughts on the newest note tells the whole story.

Bank has all of these as presets, an inflation series among them, and takes any whole value from 0 to a million million.

### Paper or plastic

Banknote paper is made mostly from cotton, which is why it feels unlike the paper in a book and survives a washing machine. Since Australia issued the first polymer note in 1988, many countries have moved to plastic, which lasts longer and allows clear windows; the Bank of England has been moving its notes to polymer since 2016. The Swiss went between the two, with a sheet of polymer sandwiched between two layers of cotton paper.

In Bank, the paper has a texture that is generated, never photographed: a fine grain by default, or laid, aged or plain.

### Bringing a series out

A new series is years in the making and is rarely released at once. The Swiss series came out one value at a time between 2016 and 2019, and old notes stay valid for a time beside the new ones. A series is replaced every ten or twenty years, mostly to stay ahead of counterfeiters, which is a good excuse for your nation to have had several.

## Why Bank is fiction only

Real banknotes are protected by law nearly everywhere, and the rules for reproducing them are strict and different in every country. The European Central Bank allows pictures of euro notes only under conditions: printed copies must be clearly larger or smaller than the real thing, and digital ones must be low in resolution and carry the word "specimen" across them. The Bank of England sets conditions of its own: printed copies show one side only and differ clearly in size, the word SPECIMEN is among the ways to mark a copy, and reproducing its notes outside the conditions is an offence. The United States asks that illustrations be one-sided and well under or over life size. On top of the law, central banks run a system, in place since 2004, that makes scanners, printers and image software refuse to handle pictures of protected notes.

None of that is a problem for a currency that doesn't exist, and Bank is built to keep it that way:

- **No real codes.** Real currencies have three-letter codes under the standard ISO 4217: two letters for the country and one for the unit, as a rule. Bank holds a list of every code in use and every code withdrawn, and refuses them all, whether typed, loaded from a file or hidden in a link. It will suggest free ones.
- **No real names or signs in our own designs.** You may call your money what you like; plenty of invented nations have a crown or a dollar. Our templates don't.
- **No uploads.** Bank takes no pictures at all, so it can't be handed a photograph of a real note or a real person.
- **A specimen mark.** Every picture carries a small SPECIMEN overprint unless you switch it off.
- **Fine print.** Every picture says "fictional currency, bank.eurth.org" in its margin. That one has no switch.

## Designing your own

1. Start with the currency: its name, its plural, a sign and a code. A name that sounds like your nation's language does more than any picture.
2. Choose a theme, and let it pick the portrait and the vignettes.
3. Pick the values. Six is plenty. Follow 1, 2, 5 unless your nation has a reason not to, and if it has, put the reason in its history.
4. Give every value a colour, and check that neighbours differ in lightness.
5. Decide who signs. The titles say a lot about a state: a Governor, a Lord High Treasurer and a People's Commissar do not work in the same building.
6. Draw one note well, then make the series from it.
7. Write down what the pictures mean. Real banks publish a page on every note; yours should too.
